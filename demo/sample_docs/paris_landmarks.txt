A Short Guide to Four Paris Landmarks

The Eiffel Tower

The Eiffel Tower is a wrought-iron lattice tower on the Champ de Mars in Paris. It was built between 1887 and 1889 as the entrance arch for the 1889 Exposition Universelle, the world's fair held to mark the centennial of the French Revolution. The tower is named after Gustave Eiffel, whose engineering company designed and built it. The original design came from two senior engineers in his company, Maurice Koechlin and Emile Nouguier, and the architect Stephen Sauvestre refined its appearance.

When it opened, the tower was about 300 metres tall, and it remained the tallest man-made structure in the world until the Chrysler Building in New York was completed in 1930. With the antennas added since then, it now stands about 330 metres high. The structure is made of roughly 18,000 individual iron parts joined by about 2.5 million rivets. Visitors can reach three levels; the top level is about 276 metres above the ground.

Many artists and writers protested against the tower while it was being built and called it an eyesore. It was originally given a permit to stand for only 20 years. It survived largely because it proved valuable as a radio transmission tower. Today it is repainted roughly every seven years, and it is one of the most visited paid monuments in the world.

The Louvre

The Louvre is the world's most-visited art museum. The building began as a fortress built in the late twelfth century and later became a royal palace. It opened as a public museum in 1793, during the French Revolution. Its collection includes the Mona Lisa by Leonardo da Vinci and the ancient Greek statue known as the Venus de Milo. The glass and metal pyramid in the main courtyard, designed by the architect I. M. Pei, was completed in 1989 and now serves as the main entrance.

Notre-Dame de Paris

Notre-Dame de Paris is a medieval Catholic cathedral on the Ile de la Cite, an island in the river Seine. Construction began in 1163, and the cathedral is considered one of the finest examples of French Gothic architecture, known for its flying buttresses, rose windows and sculpted gargoyles. On 15 April 2019 a fire destroyed the cathedral's spire and most of its roof. After five years of restoration work, Notre-Dame reopened to the public in December 2024.

The Arc de Triomphe

The Arc de Triomphe stands at the western end of the Champs-Elysees, at the centre of the Place Charles de Gaulle. It was commissioned by Napoleon in 1806, after his victory at the Battle of Austerlitz, and was completed in 1836. The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier from the First World War lies beneath the arch, and an eternal flame there is rekindled every evening.
